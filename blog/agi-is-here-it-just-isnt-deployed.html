Thesis May 2026

AGI is here.
It just isn't deployed.

The model gap closed in 2024–25. The deployment gap didn't — and the companies still waiting for a smarter model are looking in the wrong direction.

Spend an afternoon with the current frontier models and the argument about raw capability is hard to sustain. They write production code, decompose a goal into sub-tasks, search over their own reasoning, and call tools against live systems. The thing the field spent a decade chasing — broad, general, transferable competence — is, for most knowledge work, already in the room.

And yet the workflows that decide how a company competes — demand forecasting at SKU granularity, credit recalibration through the cycle, clinical trial design, supplier negotiation — are still run by people, with an assistant at the edges at most. If the model is good enough, why isn't the work being done?

Because capability was never the binding constraint. The constraint is everything that sits between a capable model and a workflow you'd actually trust it to run.

Two gaps, and only one of them closed

It helps to grade the field by maturity rather than hype. Discriminative models — speech recognition, object detection, medical imaging — are mature: production-ready, widely deployed, boring in the best sense. Generative models are mainstream: rapid adoption, quality still climbing. Agentic systems — the ones that take actions, not just produce text — are emerging: early deployment, variable reliability, human oversight still required.

That last line is the whole story. The model gap — can it reason, can it generalize — closed. The deployment gap — will it do the right thing, every time, on a workflow where being wrong is expensive — did not. These are different problems, and the second one does not get solved by a better model. It gets solved by the system around the model.

The uncomfortable part is that the benchmarks most teams cite actively hide this. A binary did it pass? score reports a single success number and tells you nothing about why the other attempts failed. Once you instrument the failures, the picture inverts.

Most agent failures are execution failures

We took tau2-bench — Sierra's agent benchmark for multi-turn, tool-using tasks in domains like airline and retail support — and rebuilt the logging around it so every tool call, every argument, every state transition is recorded. The point was not a higher score. The point was to see where the runs actually break.

They do not break on reasoning. They break on doing.

Read those again, because the order matters. The model knew what to do. It picked the right tool, formed roughly the right call, and then fumbled the execution — a malformed argument, a validation error it didn't recover from, a context window that quietly degraded as the conversation grew. None of that is a thinking problem.

So we didn't change the model. We wrapped it: a retry layer that catches a validation error and tries again with the correction, and a context layer that keeps the working set under the cliff. Action failures fell from 87% toward 45%; the 3,000-token cliff flattened; overall success roughly doubled. Same weights. Twice the reliability. All of it from the loop above the model.

The bottleneck isn't the model. It's the loop above the model — and that loop is something you build, not something you wait for.

Why this is the whole opportunity

If the gap were a model gap, the right move would be to wait — the next release is always a few months out, and someone else pays to close it. But a deployment gap doesn't close on a vendor's roadmap. It closes when a specific workflow's specific failure modes get caught, corrected, regression-tested, and never allowed to recur. That work is local. It cannot be pre-trained at a lab, because the failures that matter are yours — your edge cases, your data, your definition of a wrong answer.

This is why we think the durable asset isn't the model and isn't even the agent. It's the loop that turns yesterday's production failure into today's eval case, today's eval case into tomorrow's fix, and that fix into something a domain expert can approve without taking an engineer's word for it. The retry-and-context result above is the smallest possible version of that loop. The full version is what ownEvo builds:

  • every production failure becomes a clustered eval case;
  • every proposed fix is regression-tested against every prior fix — no step forward that loses a step already gained;
  • a domain expert, not a developer, approves the change in plain language;
  • and the whole chain is written to an audit log the customer owns and can verify.

The model you run underneath is your choice, and you can swap it next quarter. What compounds is the loop on top — and it stays yours.
